Forty-five seconds on the farm.

Four chickens, one farm, forty-five seconds. Collect food, bank it at your base, and rob everyone else blind before the clock runs out. No health bars, no grinding a rival down — every fight is about taking their food and buying time.

02 — Core loop

Farm, carry, bank, repeat — while three rivals try to stop you

The loop is deliberately short enough to run four or five times in a match. Everything interesting happens in the gap between picking food up and getting it home.

Pick

One class, one specialization, and the ability slots that choice leaves free.

Spawn

At your own corner base. Every client rotates the arena so your corner sits at the bottom of the screen.

Farm

Stand on a pile and Peck. Piles are tiered: 5 at your doorstep, 10 contested, 20 in the centre.

Carry

Cargo is capped per class. Standing on a pile slows you to ×0.80; collisions slow you to ×0.75.

Bank

Deposit at your base at 9 food/sec. Banked food is safe — and permanently out of circulation.

↻ The side loop that decides matches

At 2× supply, two players could theoretically max out by pure farming. So combat's job is to buy time, and steal is the only mechanic that converts a won fight directly into food. Catching a loaded rival mid-route beats winning a fair fight.

03 — The balance backbone

Solo Clear Time: every stat is a dependent variable

A naked chicken — no abilities, no passive, alone on the full map, farming greedily — banks the win target in a fixed time, over a fixed number of base trips. Move speed isn't 12.15 because it feels nice. It's 12.15 because Speedy must clear in 30 seconds.

ClassSCT targetTripsMoveCargoStrategy
The load-bearing detail. Speedy and Assassin share the exact same move speed (12.15) and the exact same cargo (10) — yet one clears in 30s and the other in 40s. The entire gap is foraging rate. The Assassin is a fast chicken who is genuinely bad at farming, and that is the mechanical reason he denies instead of collects.
Verified, not asserted. A pure-C# simulator (BalanceOracle) runs the greedy farming policy against the real pile coordinates and asserts each class lands within ±1.5s of its target. Trips are ceil(40 / CargoCapacity) — which is why Fatty's 35 cargo buys him two trips and Speedy's 10 buys four.

06 — Slot rules

Two slots are decided for you. The rest are yours.

Every class gets exactly four active slots — no class has five buttons. Two of them are determined rather than chosen: one answers to your class, one to your specialization. Everything left over you pick freely.

Determined by class

The forager slot

Warrior, Speedy and Fatty each carry their own class's Peck — the amount and cooldown are authored per class, so this is already a per-class variant rather than one shared button. The Assassin has no forager at all, which is the mechanical reason it cannot farm.

Determined by specialization

The signature slot

Every specialization grants exactly one signature ability, equipped automatically. This is what makes the specialization choice read as a build rather than a stat tweak — you are choosing a button, not a modifier.

Chosen by you

The rest

A forager always picks 2; the Assassin, having no forager slot to spend, always picks 3. Free picks come from that class's own pool plus the two Common abilities. Slot pressure never depends on which specialization you took.

→

Free picks — this class's legal pool

Three specializations have no signature assigned yet. The Agile, the Anxious and the Hauler currently grant their passive only — the one place where “every specialization determines a signature” is not yet literally true. They were previously earmarked for Peck variants, but that plan was retired on 2026-09-04: under this model the forager slot answers to the class, so a specialization cannot grant one. The per-class foraging variation those variants wanted already exists in each class's authored Peck values. They need ordinary abilities instead.

07 — Chicken versus chicken

Steal and control are the whole combat system

There is no basic attack, no HP and no damage. Encounters are decided by ability choice, cooldown management and positioning.

The control ladder

Why stun is special. Because it is the only state that locks casting, it is the qualifier for the Assassin's execute. Stun exists in exactly two classes — Assassin and Warrior — which is what makes the deny/enable counterplay reachable. Slow, root and stun are independent and can all be active at once.

The steal formula

A loaded thief cannot steal

stolen = min(abilityStealValue, attackerFreeSpace, defenderCargo)

4 food transferred
Cargo transfers directly, attacker to victim. Nothing ever drops on the floor. The formula self-regulates: a full thief steals zero, so theft is a rhythm — steal, bank, return — not a drain. And Fatty's 35 cargo can only leave in bites of 3 to 6, so under pressure he bleeds instead of popping. His defensive abilities are what he is actually playing.

08 — The only hard removal

Mark / Kill: one button, two presses

A chicken is never killed — except by this. It is the Reaper specialization's forced signature, so choosing Reaper equips it automatically. The Burglar does not carry it at all and can never execute.

MarkPRESS 1

Soft-locks the nearest isolated rival in facing — no chicken within 8 m. Visible to the target.

CounterplayStay near others. In an FFA, safety means approaching an enemy.

CounterplayBreak isolation or leave range and the mark fizzles.

The marked target enters a stun — his own Ambush, or any stun, including a Warrior's Wing Slam. The button relabels to KILL.

CounterplayDon't get stunned. Hold a defensive ability.

KillPRESS 2

Fires only while the target is stunned and the Assassin is not himself stunned.

CounterplayA third party can stun the Assassin to deny it. Stun the hunter to save the prey.
On success the victim's entire cargo transfers to the Assassin as a capacity-exempt bounty bag — he becomes a courier carrying a stolen win, far past his own 10-cargo limit. The victim is removed for about two seconds and respawns empty. On failure Mark/Kill takes a reduced cooldown, so he re-marks fast.

09 — The arena

A 51.3 m square you can never see all of

Four bases, one per corner. Eight radial walls on the boundaries of eight 45° sectors. Nine food piles in three tiers. The arena is about 1.9 screens edge-to-edge — that information gap is deliberate, and it is what makes routing and ambush matter.

Hover or tap any pile, base or wall for its shipped values.

Open problem — known, accepted for now, must be solved

The arena is too big for four players in forty-five seconds

In play, four chickens on a 51.3 m square rarely run into each other. Routes are long enough that a whole match can pass in parallel — everyone farms, everyone banks, and the encounter the entire v0.4 redesign exists to produce never happens. The control-and-steal systems go unexercised, which means the part of the game we spent the redesign on is the part players see least.

This is a density problem, not a layout problem. The topology on the map above — two laps, eight wedges, tiered piles — is sound and measured. There is simply too much floor per chicken per second.

Shrink the arena

Cheapest, but every spatial constant derives from arena half-size, and the last rescale held clear time constant by moving speeds with it. Shrinking alone shortens clear time unless speeds come down too.

Pull the objectives inward

Reduce the contested and doorstep radii so routes overlap sooner, without touching arena size or the sightline contract.

Raise the player count

The arena may simply be sized for six to eight. Changes the free-for-all maths and the win target, so it is the most expensive option.

Funnel traffic with geometry

More interior structure forcing shared corridors. The scatter system already implements a barrier role — it ships at zero.

Why it is parked. We do not yet know whether the encounter rate is wrong or the readability of encounters is wrong, and guessing costs a re-solve of every derived constant. This is the first question a human playtest has to answer.

The two laps

The pattern is 4-fold symmetric, so every player's sector is identical: an outer-gap wall on one side, an inner-gap wall on the other. Combined with per-player rotation that becomes one universal, learnable rule — left is the safe lap (out to the rim, along the edge, past a contested pile) and right is the risky lap (in past the hub plaza and the centre pile).

Why exactly eight wedges

Not a round number — a proven optimum. Every pile and base sits on a multiple of 45°, so the offset between a wall and the nearest objective is a pure function of the wedge count. A wall closer than 11.665° to an objective gets clipped away by its keep-clear disc. W=8 gives 22.5° — the maximum the topology allows. W=12 and W=20 land four arms exactly on the corner diagonals; W=16 misses by 0.415°. Raising the count adds arms that the objectives then delete.

Piles never respawn

Banked food leaves circulation permanently, so the pot visibly shrinks and the late game gets desperate on its own — no endgame rule required. Fatty's winning route (a 35-cargo sweep of centre plus two contested piles) is forced through the contested ring: he cannot win without walking past everyone.

10 — Getting over things

All jumps are teleports

There are no obstacle height classes. Whether a jump clears something depends only on its length versus the obstacle's span along the jump direction, plus 0.8 m of body clearance. Three tiers ship. Pick one.

What this tier clears, square on

Failure is not a no-op. If the landing point is blocked, you travel as far as you can and stop flush against the near face — you slam into it rather than failing in place. The cooldown is still spent. A small tolerance (max(12%, 0.4 m), hard-capped at 0.8 m) extends a jump that falls only slightly short, so long jumps can't quietly gain reach.

Emergent late game

The ring becomes a circle

The centre pile's footprint shrinks with its remaining food. Since span is the only thing gating traversal, the map opens itself in stages with no extra systems — the prize everyone is fighting over is also the wall protecting it, so the arena dissolves exactly as the match converges.

Balance-critical, not cosmetic. How fast it opens is a tuning curve on the pile's shrink rate. A linear footprint would end the fortress phase at around 75% remaining, which is likely far too early.

11 — Game feel

Hold to aim, release to fire

The rule that matters is short: an ability always fires on release, never on press. There is no tap window and no branch. Holding does not charge power. What the hold buys you is information — and it costs you mobility.

Quick tap < 120 msDeliberate hold ≥ 120 ms
Ground telegraphnoneYour true area, drawn
Target marksnoneEvery chicken in the area classified — will-be-hit vs. immune
Wind-up glow rivals seenoneRamps up at your feet
MovementFull speedThe stick rotates your aim instead of moving you
Latency to fireOn releaseOn release — identical
The asymmetry is the point. You see your true area; rivals see only a growing glow at your feet — a wind-up tell, not your shape. That preserves the read (“he's charging something, disengage”) without turning the arena into a solved puzzle. And because the glow now only appears when someone is genuinely committing to an aim, it means something again — it used to fire on every press, so rivals learned to ignore it.

Why a button refuses — four reasons, told apart at a glance

On cooldownDarkens from the bottom up, seconds remaining in the centre.

Pressing a refused button shakes it and clicks. A press is never silently swallowed. Cancelling a held ability — dragging past ~55 dp, or Esc on desktop — releases without burning the cooldown; losing pointer capture to an OS gesture counts as a cancel, never a fire.

12 — Technology

What it is built on, and why

A mobile-first networked action game has two hard constraints: it must simulate identically on every peer, and it must hold frame rate on a five-year-old Android phone. Nearly every choice below is downstream of one of those two.

Engine & rendering

Unity 6000.3 LTS
  • Universal Render Pipeline. Mobile-tier lighting and post with a single pass budget. URP materials only — a Built-in RP shader silently breaks the build target.
  • MonoBehaviour, not ECS/DOTS. Deliberate: four chickens and nine piles never stress the object model, and DOTS would cost far more in networking complexity than it returns.
  • 2.5D. Real 3D meshes on an orthographic isometric camera — no perspective distortion, so silhouettes stay readable at any position on screen.
  • Targets. 60 fps Windows, a stable 30 on mid-range Android from 2021 onward.

Networking

Photon Fusion 2 · Shared Mode
  • Shared Mode for the demo. Each peer holds state authority over its own chicken; the master client owns scene objects — food piles, bases, the match manager.
  • Every cross-authority write is an RPC. [Rpc(RpcSources.All, RpcTargets.StateAuthority)], with the target validating range, cooldown and legality before applying. The caster's word is never final.
  • Simulation runs at a 30 Hz tick. Prediction for the local player is automatic; snapshots reconcile silently behind it.
  • Forward-compatible with Server Mode. Every state write is already gated on state authority, so moving authority server-side post-funding changes the host, not the shape of the code.

Architecture

Zenject · interface-first
  • Two DI scopes. An app-wide container that survives scene loads, and a scene container for match-lifetime bindings.
  • Everything external sits behind an interface — networking, backend, audio, input, logging. Gameplay code never touches Fusion, Unity Gaming Services or the Input System directly, which is what makes the demo runnable with null implementations.
  • Self-injection for networked objects. Fusion spawns outside the DI graph, so networked behaviours inject themselves on spawn rather than being constructed by the container.
  • Data lives in ScriptableObjects. Classes, abilities, match config, palette and prefab registries are all authored assets — which is why this page can be generated from them.

Balance & testing

EditMode suite
  • The Balance Oracle. A pure-C# simulator with no Unity types, running the greedy farming policy against the real pile coordinates. Class stats are solved against it, never hand-tuned.
  • Design rules are tests. Speedy's traversal ban, the eight-wedge optimum, the wall-versus-pile clearances and the traversal matrix are all pinned by EditMode tests that fail with the reason in the message.
  • Roster integrity is checked live in an editor window, so an ability drifting to a second class is caught while authoring rather than in review.

Platforms & UI

Windows · Android · iOS
  • Mobile-first input. Left-thumb virtual joystick sets both movement and facing; four right-thumb ability buttons. Everything works on a touchscreen first, desktop second.
  • UI Toolkit only for menus. UXML and USS, no procedural UGUI — the older AI-generated prefab paths were deleted outright rather than left as a second way to do the same thing.
  • Windows is primary dev, Android is the target. iOS ships as an Xcode export, off by default.

Backend & tooling

UGS · MCP bridge
  • Unity Gaming Services behind one interface for auth, lobbies and relay, with a null implementation so the demo runs with no backend at all.
  • An MCP bridge into the Editor. Agents inspect scenes, query assets, edit components, take screenshots and run the test suite directly — which is how the data on this page was read.
  • In-game diagnostics. A debug HUD on F1, structured log sources, and device logs over adb.

13 — How it gets built

AI does most of the typing. The rules decide whether that works.

Cluck Wars is built by a one-person studio running a roster of specialized agents. That only holds together because the project is organised around a single assumption: an agent will confidently do the wrong thing unless the right thing is written down next to the code it governs, and checked by something that runs.

Organization

One orchestrator, fifteen specialists, and a mandatory review

Work does not go straight to an implementer. Every non-trivial request enters through an architecture-owning agent that decomposes it, routes it, and then reviews what comes back. No agent marks its own work done.

Step 1

Request

A task in plain language, from the one human on the project.

Step 2

Triage

The architect reads current state and recent history, decides scope, and picks the specialist. It never implements multi-file changes itself.

Step 3

Execute

One specialist owns the change, working to a written spec rather than the original prompt.

Step 4

Review

The architect, or a dedicated review agent for networking-heavy diffs, checks the result against the conventions. Failures go back to step 3.

Step 5

Record

State doc updated, decision written down, one concern per commit.

Orchestration

Owns structure and signoff. The default entry point.

Why split art into five agents instead of one. A render-pipeline problem and a ShaderGraph problem look identical in a bug report — “the game looks washed out” — and have completely different fixes. Narrow agents fail visibly instead of confidently guessing outside their competence, which is the failure mode that actually costs time.

Rules

Constraints written where the agent will trip over them

A convention that lives only in a chat log is not a convention. These are checked into the repo next to the code, and the load-bearing ones are also tests.

Everything external sits behind an interface

Gameplay code never touches the networking library, the backend, or the input system directly. The reason is agentic: an agent reaching for the concrete API is an easy thing to spot in review, whereas subtly wrong lifecycle usage is not.

Authority gates are non-negotiable

Every networked update early-returns unless it owns the state; every cross-authority write goes through an RPC. Stated once, repeated in the specialist's own instructions, and enforced in review.

The silent-failure sorting rule

Settled after four byte-identical silent guards were found across the placed-zone abilities. Ask whether the null could be produced by a legal game state. Unassigned field or missing component → a wiring bug that must be logged loudly. Nobody in range, cargo full, nothing to steal → the game working, and it must stay silent. A sweep that logs every early return is worse than the bug.

Design decisions are tests

Speedy's traversal ban, the eight-wedge optimum, the wall clearances and the traversal matrix are all pinned by tests that fail with the reason in the message. A rule an agent can read but not violate is worth more than one it merely agrees with.

Write the decision next to the thing it governs

Adopted after a month in which every drift bug traced to intent that lived only in chat. If a class may never receive traversal abilities, that sentence belongs in the class document and in a test — not in a conversation nobody will read again.

One concern per commit, and the body explains why

Agents happily produce “fix bug + add feature + refactor” mega-commits. The subject says what changed; the body says why, because the why is the part no future agent can reconstruct from the diff. Agent contributions carry co-author attribution.

What went wrong, and what it changed

Four failures that every check in place passed

These are the ones worth publishing. Each was invisible to the tooling that existed at the time, and each changed a rule rather than just a line of code.

Missing reference, not a code path

Two abilities rendered nothing at all

The component that draws a zone's edge ring was never added to the zone prefab, so a 3.6 m, 5-second slow cloud had no tell whatsoever — you learned where a trap was by already being slowed inside it. The feedback doc had both cases marked shipped. No test could catch it: an unassigned prefab reference is not a code path.

Changed: prefab wiring became something review checks explicitly, and the silent-failure rule got its "wiring bug" branch.

Confident, plausible, and wrong

Every wing animation in the game was a no-op

A docblock claimed wings animate on one axis and that this was “measured off the shipped clips, not guessed”. It was the roll axis — rotating it leaves the wingtip exactly where it started. Five of eight cast archetypes were mutually indistinguishable. Every per-frame assertion passed, because a wing that never moves cannot violate a relationship.

Changed: assertions now test features and relationships that would break if the motion stopped, not bounding boxes that survive it.

Data drifted away from intent

A class silently gained a forbidden ability

An asset serialized a class mask granting Speedy a blink that crossed every obstacle in the game — against a constructor, a class docstring and a design section that all said otherwise. The intent existed in three places. None of them were the asset.

Changed: a test now sweeps the whole roster for it, and the rule that produced it is written in the design doc it governs.

The test agreed with itself

A balance drift stayed green for weeks

The harness that verifies every class hits its clear-time target was feeding the simulator hardcoded stats instead of loading the shipped assets. A 20% speed change landed and the suite never noticed, because it was checking the numbers in the test against the numbers in the test.

Changed: balance tests read the real assets. A test that does not touch shipped data is not verifying shipped behaviour.

The through-line. None of these were reasoning failures — they were verification failures. In every case the agent produced something plausible, the checks in place agreed, and the gap only closed when a rule was written somewhere a machine could enforce it. That is the actual lesson of building this way: the model is rarely the bottleneck, and the harness around it almost always is.

14 — Design notes

Decisions worth defending

A compendium of mechanics is only half the story. These are the calls that shaped them, including the ones that were wrong the first time.

Constraint

Speedy never gets terrain traversal

Not a jump, not a blink, not a vault. Speedy already answers two currencies alone: escape (highest move speed, multiplied further by Speed Burst) and denial (three control abilities). Traversal would be a third with nothing traded for it, and the map would stop constraining the fastest chicken in the game. The line is no ignoring terrain, not no mobility — which is why Speed Burst is deliberately legal.

This rule had already been broken once by asset drift: Shadowstep serialized a class mask including Speedy against a constructor, a docstring and the design doc that all said Assassin. An EditMode test now sweeps the whole roster for it.

Correction

A margin that was 24× larger than believed

The wall layout was documented as surviving pile keep-clear discs by about 0.15 m — terrifyingly tight. That figure had been computed against a pre-shrink pile footprint at a pre-rescale radius: a map that stopped shipping months earlier. Measured against what actually ships, the margin is +3.656 m. The lesson isn't the number, it's that a derived constant outlived the geometry it was derived from and nothing caught it.

Consolidation

Abilities stopped being shared

Speed Burst and Snatch used to live in the Common pool. Both turned out to be class essences hiding in shared clothing — a universal sprint erases Speedy's whole “fastest in the game” claim, and a universal AoE steal is the Assassin's entire income. Widening loadouts had cost readability. The Common pool is now exactly two abilities.

Deliberate weakness

Some abilities are worse on purpose

Headbutt is deliberately weaker than Cluck Shock — the shortest cooldown in the Warrior kit pays for it. Ruffle is deliberately weaker than Speed Burst. Scrap is deliberately weaker than Sneaky Steal. Each pairing is a class identity statement, not a balance oversight: the weaker version is the one whose class doesn't own that fantasy.

Known gap

Simplifying the slot rules retired a plan

The old model let slot pressure vary by specialization, and nobody could hold it in their head. Restating it as class decides the forager, specialization decides the signature fixed the confusion — and killed the plan for the three unassigned signatures in the same move, since they were all going to be Peck variants and a specialization can no longer grant a forager. The variation those variants wanted turned out to already exist, in each class's own authored Peck values. Three specializations now need ordinary abilities instead.

Flagged

Wing Slam contradicts its own class

At a 12-second cooldown it is the longest in the game — sitting on the class whose essence is spamming abilities. It is flagged for re-tuning rather than quietly left alone. Similarly, Turtle Mode and Roll & Push still carry flavour text mentioning “damage”, left over from the pre-v0.4 combat model. The mechanics are correct; only the copy is stale. Both are marked in the codex above.
